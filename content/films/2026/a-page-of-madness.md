A former sailor takes a job as a janitor at the asylum where his wife is confined, hoping to free her. What follows is less a story than a fever: dancers who never stop dancing, corridors that bend, faces dissolving into superimposition. There are no intertitles. Nothing explains itself.

Made in 1926 by Teinosuke Kinugasa with a scenario developed alongside Yasunari Kawabata — four decades before Kawabata became Japan's first Nobel laureate in literature — *A Page of Madness* was Japanese cinema's collision with European avant-garde montage, and almost nobody saw it. It was believed destroyed, one more casualty of a national archive lost to fire and war.

Then in 1971, after forty-five years, Kinugasa found a print in a rice barrel in his own storage shed.

What survives is missing close to a third of what screened in 1926, and no score has ever been definitive: in its own time a *benshi* narrator would have performed it live, differently every night. That makes every screening a reconstruction. This is a film that has never twice been the same thing, and it is a century old this year.
